# Lesson 01: Interview Fundamentals

## Learning Objectives
- Understand the standard tech interview process and its various stages.
- Differentiate between interview formats (phone screen, technical, system design, behavioral, take-home).
- Develop a comprehensive and sustainable preparation strategy.

## 1. Why the Tech Interview Exists
Tech interviews often feel broken or disconnected from day-to-day work. Why do companies ask you to invert a binary tree when you're applying for a React role? 

**The Mental Model:**
The tech interview process is fundamentally an **imperfection risk mitigation strategy** for companies. Hiring a bad candidate (false positive) is much more expensive than missing out on a good candidate (false negative). Therefore, the process is designed to filter out risks rather than perfectly identify potential.

**Real-world Analogy:**
Think of the tech interview like a standardized test (e.g., SAT, GRE). It doesn't perfectly measure your ability to succeed in the real world, but it provides a common baseline that interviewers can use to compare candidates objectively across different backgrounds.

## 2. Standard Interview Process

Most mid-to-large tech companies follow a similar pipeline:

1. **Recruiter Screen (15-30 mins):** Checking for basic alignment (salary expectations, timeline, work authorization).
2. **Technical Screen (45-60 mins):** A quick technical check, often a coding question or a brief take-home, to ensure you meet the minimum technical bar.
3. **Onsite/Virtual Loop (4-6 hours):** A series of 45-60 minute interviews typically consisting of:
   - 2x Coding / Data Structures & Algorithms
   - 1x System Design (for Mid/Senior roles)
   - 1x Behavioral / Cultural Fit

## 3. Interview Formats Explained

### A. The Coding Interview (Whiteboard/CoderPad)
- **What it is:** Solving algorithmic or domain-specific problems in real-time.
- **What they look for:** Problem-solving ability, code cleanliness, edge-case identification, communication, and time complexity analysis.
- **Best Practice:** The "Think Out Loud" protocol. Your thought process is more important than a perfectly compiling solution. 

### B. The System Design Interview
- **What it is:** Architecting a large-scale system (e.g., "Design Twitter").
- **What they look for:** Ability to handle ambiguity, understanding of scale, trade-offs, database choices, caching, and APIs.
- **Best Practice:** Drive the conversation. Ask clarifying questions first (requirements gathering), then propose a high-level design before deep-diving.

### C. Behavioral Interview
- **What it is:** Questions about your past experiences (e.g., "Tell me about a time you had a conflict with a coworker").
- **What they look for:** Soft skills, leadership, conflict resolution, ownership, and alignment with company values.
- **Best Practice:** Use the **STAR method** (Situation, Task, Action, Result).

### D. Take-Home Assignments
- **What it is:** Building a small application or feature on your own time (typically 3-5 hours).
- **What they look for:** Production-ready code, testing, Git history, architecture, and ability to follow requirements.
- **Best Practice:** Treat it like a real production task. Write a good README, add tests, and use a linter.

## 4. Preparation Strategy

Don't just grind LeetCode blindly. Follow a structured approach:

1. **Assess Your Weaknesses:** Are you failing phone screens? Focus on algorithms. Failing loops? Focus on system design or behavioral.
2. **Spaced Repetition:** Review concepts regularly rather than cramming.
3. **Mock Interviews:** The biggest gap is often communication, not technical knowledge. Practice with peers on platforms like Pramp.
